# A Deeper Story of Chinese Manufacturing: Lessons from ZX Moto

> Source: [Xinhua News Agency / Xinhua Daily Telegraph](https://www.news.cn/20261019/dfbeb67220bf44f280c286ef7f3820fa/c.html)

## A Deeper Story of Chinese Manufacturing: Lessons from ZX Moto

On October 18, the 2026 Superbike World Championship (WSBK) season ended in Jerez, Spain. French rider Valentin Debise of Chinese manufacturer ZX Moto finished runner-up in the WorldSSP riders' championship, while ZX Moto placed third in the class's manufacturers' standings.

September 19: Visitors view ZX Moto at the 24th China International Motorcycle Trade Exhibition. Unless otherwise noted, photographs in this series are by Xinhua Daily Telegraph reporter Huang Wei.

Jerez is an old circuit demanding strong braking and corner exits. Along the pit-lane passage stand Yamaha blue, Ducati red, Kawasaki green, and Triumph yellow. Behind each color is an "industrial dynasty" lasting half a century or even more than a hundred years. Amid them is a two-year-old Chinese name: ZX Moto.

Registered in Chongqing's Liangjiang New Area in April 2024, the company saw its motorcycle win six times across eleven rounds two years later, placing third in the manufacturers' standings.

The light of victory illuminates more than a racing motorcycle's speed. What looks like a thrilling success story is actually the result of more than forty years of catching up: on the world's narrowest racetrack, Chinese manufacturing fitted its own heart for the first time and won.

The real questions are: Why did one person's breakthrough happen in Chongqing? How could a two-year-old Chinese racing manufacturer defeat century-old powerhouses? What stands behind a young man who forgoes short-term profits and sustains heavy R&D investment? Having won on track, how can it win in the market?

Lesson One: A Complete Supply Chain Supports One Person's Persistence

In 2024, Zhang meets staff at ZX Moto in Chongqing's Liangjiang New Area to discuss product details.

Born in 1987 in a remote village in Mayang County, Huaihua, Hunan, Zhang left school as a teenager and became an apprentice at a county-town motorcycle shop, growing up among engine oil and wrenches. He later developed a special skill: reassembling an engine blindfolded.

In 2006, nineteen-year-old Zhang saw motorcycle rallying on television and formed a goal: one day, he would win on the world stage. He repeatedly called Hunan Satellite TV, saying he rode well and wanted to appear. When the crew came, he repeatedly crashed. Thinking his ability unremarkable, they decided to leave.

As their vehicles departed, he mounted a battered old motorcycle and followed. Through heavy rain, he pursued them for over three hours and more than 100 kilometers, seeking only a chance on camera. When they finally filmed him, the soaked teenager shouted: "Courage makes my life more exciting!" At twenty, he joined a team in Siyang, Jiangsu, as a stunt rider and mechanic, also recognizing the industry's weakness: enormous output without the technological soul at its core.

In 2013, he went alone to Chongqing with RMB 20,000 in savings.

In Chongqing's repair circles, he became a renowned craftsman, having dismantled engines countless times and able to identify parts by touch. In 2017, he helped found Kove. Its first independently developed 800cc twin-cylinder engine fired successfully in 2021; in 2023, he led its team to the Dakar Rally start line.

In late February 2024, he posted a resignation letter on WeChat Moments: "I have decided to resign to pursue my stars and seas."

April 1: Staff perform complete-vehicle final inspections at ZX Moto's Liangjiang New Area workshop.

Two months later, a brand bearing his own name was established in Chongqing. It had raised only RMB 38 million. He calculated the budget day by day: "If the product was not on sale by March 2025, our funding chain would break." A team of a dozen or so ate and slept at the factory, dismantling and reassembling engines through countless failures and fresh starts.

The market answered that persistence with real money. His first model, the 500RR, sold over 20,000 units in a year, turning a brand that barely had time to explain itself into a hit.

March 2026 truly brought it before the world. Two victories at Portugal's Portimão circuit made it famous; 820RR orders rose 158% in twenty-four hours and 200% over three days in Chongqing, validating the classic "win on Sunday, sell on Monday" rule. Capital followed: from a RMB 100 million angel-round valuation in 2024 to RMB 6 billion after HongShan entered in August 2026. What impressed investors was Zhang's almost obsessive product commitment.

People often choose a city because it contains something capable of sustaining their dreams. He came alone neither for subsidies nor for investment-promotion promises, but for its industry cluster. From a screw to an engine, once a drawing was ready, a supplier willing to prototype could be found that day. This environment gave an entrepreneur the greatest luxury: speed in trial and error.

April 1: Staff inspect complete motorcycles awaiting export at ZX Moto's Liangjiang New Area factory.

Today, Chongqing has sixty motorcycle manufacturers and 431 component makers above the designated size, with combined annual capacity exceeding 20 million complete motorcycles and 20 million engines. Local sourcing exceeds 80% for fuel-powered bikes. Every component, from screw to engine, is available within a "one-hour industry circle." At the 2026 Summer Davos opening, the cluster was cited as an example of China's specialized, differentiated, distinctive industrial clusters. This deep foundation enabled Zhang's team to dismantle and reassemble engines countless times and iterate toward a production model within two years.

Individual breakthroughs never start from nothing; they stand on an entire supply chain. Without this soil, a person's stubbornness remains mere obstinacy; with it, it becomes perseverance. That soil absorbs the time cost of experimentation. This is what Chongqing offers all entrepreneurs: possibilities rather than promises.

Lesson Two: Core Technology Gives a Brand Confidence

Understanding Zhang's victory requires a longer view of almost half a century of industrial change in Chongqing.

Zhang was not the beginning. In 1979, Jialing Machinery Factory engineers dismantled a foreign motorcycle, measured every part, and reconstructed a 50cc engine through hand-drawn designs, before Zhang was born. That National Day, China's first civilian motorcycle, the Jialing CJ50, entered industrial history. Jialing and Jianshe later introduced Honda and Yamaha technology, while Lifan, Zongshen, and Loncin emerged from repair shops and workshops, creating a contest among "five tigers" alongside the two state firms. By the late 1990s, Chongqing had nearly 600 industrial component enterprises. Around 2000, it became the "motorcycle capital," making one in every four motorcycles produced in China.

April 1: Inspected motorcycles stand in neat rows at ZX Moto's Liangjiang New Area production workshop.

Chongqing was already familiar with world championships. In 2002, China's Zongshen team won the overall Endurance World Championship, achieving Chinese motorcycling's first world title. But the victory depended on piecing things together. To survive twenty-four hours of high loads, the team searched everywhere: engine mounts painstakingly made domestically, suspension bought in Italy, brakes sourced in Japan. They won, yet there was no truly Chinese motorcycle.

The 1.0 era emphasized quantity over refinement. Engines were copied, fuel-injection systems purchased, and products focused on basic transport with competition through lower prices. Li Xueping, vice president of the Chongqing Automobile and Motorcycle Industry Association, put it directly: "However large the volume, we still could not stand tall." That was the root of Chongqing motorcycling's scale without strength.

In the 2.0 era, the question became whether Chongqing could build well. The answer: first build the heart.

April 1: Staff work on the final assembly line at ZX Moto's Liangjiang New Area workshop.

If the supply chain is the skeleton, core technology is the heart. The 820RR-RS that won a Chinese manufacturer's historic first victory at Portimão uses an independently developed three-cylinder engine, revving beyond 15,000rpm and delivering 153.6 horsepower. Its production 820RR has 97% local content, with the engine, electronics, and chassis core systems 100% independently developed. In Chongqing, Zongshen Industrial Group chairman Zuo Zongshen watched the finish replay on his phone in prolonged silence. Describing the move from "assembling parts" to "building hearts," he said: "Today's fully domestic chain is underpinned by the rise of all Chinese manufacturing." This was not a lone hero's solo performance, but an industrial relay across generations.

"Without independent engine development, there is no way forward!" Zhang's words express a builder's obsession with core technology. It has a price: in 2025, ZX Moto devoted 9.33% of output value, about RMB 69.58 million, to R&D, recording a loss of RMB 22.78 million. He also disclosed that he personally still owed the company RMB 30 million: "Many call me a billionaire. I am negative—negative as in debt."

The other return is confidence that withstands scrutiny. After the first victory, critics called it "a flash in the pan" and "just coincidence." There was one response: keep winning. A final-lap strike in Hungary, two more wins in the Czech Republic, and a last-lap pass at Emilia-Romagna followed. Chinese internet users repeatedly rewrote the mocking phrase: a flash twice, three times... finally "six flashes in the pan." The words remained, but their meaning reversed. Winning once might be luck. What about six times?

Zhang is far from alone in making breakthroughs. Loncin's Voge launched the industry's first full-stack two-wheeler smart-technology platform; Zongshen's E-Clutch electronic clutch reached an advanced international level; CFMOTO presented a V4 prototype producing over 210 horsepower. At September's 24th China International Motorcycle Trade Exhibition in Chongqing, 1,120 companies displayed 67 advanced technologies, twenty filling domestic gaps. Vehicle domain controllers, millimeter-wave radar, smart suspension, automotive-grade chips: technologies once belonging to cars are rapidly moving into motorcycles. Competition is shifting from displacement to electronics, from catching up to defining the field. Each builds its own bikes while all climb the same slope. China's progress involves the whole chain advancing and many approaches flourishing.

Core technology cannot be bought or traded for. Scale can be accumulated through imported capability, but the heart must be built independently. Scale without a heart essentially holds production capacity for others: however many orders arrive, pricing power remains elsewhere. Chongqing's shift from quantity to quality, and from price competition to value competition, is a slice of China's wider manufacturing upgrade.

Lesson Three: Institutional Support Sustains a Company's Long-Term Commitment

An 819cc three-cylinder engine firing up appears to happen only in a company's laboratory. Zoom out, and a whole system of institutional support becomes visible behind it.

In 2023, Chongqing included high-end motorcycles among its six distinctive industrial clusters targeting the hundred-billion-yuan scale. In 2024, it released the Action Plan for High-Quality Development of Chongqing's High-End Motorcycle Industry Cluster (2023–2027), setting a 2027 output target of RMB 140 billion. The policy direction is clear: subsidize upgrading rather than scale.

A particularly persuasive item appears in recent growth-support measures: motorcycle companies entering internationally renowned competitions such as WSBK and MotoGP can receive up to RMB 2 million based on actual homologation and team-operation spending. A local government is willing to help pay for companies to enter the world's top racing arenas. The reason is simple: global recognition requires Chinese brands to win on competitors' home ground under their rules.

Precision in service determines how quickly firms can move. Liangjiang New Area planned nearly 200 mu of industrial land for ZX Moto's new production base and implemented the "chain chief" system and "dual commissioner" mechanism, raising daily capacity from over 100 to over 200 units. On September 23, the project, planned for 500,000 motorcycles annually and covering complete-vehicle manufacturing and independently developed engines, officially landed in Lianglu Guoyuangang Comprehensive Bonded Zone. Once fully operational, it can generate annual output at the ten-billion-yuan scale and attract nearly a hundred supporting upstream and downstream firms. Zhang said at the Canton Fair that the new factory would reach 500,000 annually, with "smarter manufacturing and more controllable quality."

In 2024, Zhang, center, discusses product details with staff at ZX Moto's Liangjiang New Area premises.

Certification also receives support. Chongqing coordinates national testing bodies such as China Merchants Testing Vehicle Technology, using a closed loop of R&D, testing, and optimization to shorten development. It promotes "one test, recognition in multiple countries" and mutual-recognition partnerships between local testing institutions and overseas certifiers. Since 2024, China Merchants Testing Vehicle Technology has completed export certification testing for over 1,400 motorcycle products, and Zongshen Industrial Group's testing center has obtained authorized testing qualifications from TÜV Rheinland. For a new company eager to sell in more than a dozen countries, this saves critically important time.

Ecosystem coordination allows established trees and new seedlings to grow together. Loncin's high-end motorcycle "industry brain" serves nearly 700 supply-chain companies, over 1,800 sales outlets, and more than 400,000 owners. Two Chongqing motorcycle companies have built "future factories," and digital production lines account for 68%. Leaders such as Zongshen and Loncin set the direction through scale and technology, while newcomers such as ZX Moto use the complete chain to break through in niches, forming a pattern of "leaders guiding and newcomers breaking through." Zhang said: "Chongqing's full-lifecycle, finely tailored services let businesses concentrate on R&D and market expansion."

Good industrial policy does not share businesses' risks for them; it lowers the cost of doing difficult but right things. Its weight lies in where support goes, rather than how much. Subsidizing upgrades moves money from output volume to capability. Helping pay for an international competition acknowledges that winning under a rival's rules is more persuasive than self-certification. Allowing companies time and losses creates a window for long-term commitment. Government must step back slightly for markets to move forward.

May 30, 2023: Zhang competes in the Minfeng County, Hotan stage of the 2023 China Taklimakan International Rally and Xinjiang round of the China Cross-Country Rally Championship. Photo by Xinhua reporter Hu Huhu.

Lesson Four: Open Corridors Connect an Industry to Global Markets

How do you win in the market after winning on track? A motorcycle must sell after it is built. A racing victory earns one round of applause; converting it into lasting revenue depends on channels.

In June, over 200 ZX Moto motorcycles departed Chongqing's Guoyuan Port for Poland via Shanghai and ocean transport, the brand's first bulk export to Europe. Another 330 later went to Spain. In August, the 500RR received EU Whole Vehicle Type Approval. Just over three months separated the victories and shipments of complete motorcycles to Europe.

"Declaring and exporting directly at Guoyuan Port is very convenient. River–sea intermodal transport in particular lets our motorcycles enter international markets with a lighter burden," said foreign-trade department head Huang Qin.

September 19: Zhang addresses the new-model launch at the 24th China International Motorcycle Trade Exhibition. Photo by Xinhua reporter Chen Cheng.

Behind that lighter burden is a rewritten customs process. Previously, goods went along the Yangtze to coastal ports before ocean shipment; the domestic leg alone took fifteen to twenty days. Chongqing-made products often took ten to fifteen days longer than competitors to reach overseas customers from factory loading. Today, Chongqing Port Customs uses multimodal transport for "one declaration, one document throughout," eliminating repeated customs-transit procedures. The Chongqing–Ningbo train cuts travel to fifty hours. In 2025, it carried 14,000 TEU of motorcycle cargo, about one-third of its total freight.

Behind a brand going global is a city going global. Chongqing borders neither another country nor the sea, yet has turned that disadvantage into corridors: the New International Land-Sea Trade Corridor, China–Europe freight trains, and the Yangtze waterway connect seamlessly. Guoyuan Port links China–Europe trains with the land-sea corridor. An inland city has made itself a gateway to the sea. Subsidies can reduce a freight bill, but cannot eliminate a delay of more than ten days. Chongqing has turned a transport corridor into a predictable delivery time.

August 12: ZX Moto's Liangjiang New Area production workshop. Photo by Xinhua reporter Chen Cheng.

Together, these measures explain the assessment: "When corridors flow smoothly, industry prospers."

Today, Chongqing-made motorcycles and components reach nearly 200 countries and regions. In 2025, the city exported 6.109 million motorcycles worth RMB 26.47 billion. One in every three motorcycles exported nationally was made in Chongqing, and five of China's top ten exporters were based there. Motorcycle and knocked-down kit transport on the New International Land-Sea Trade Corridor rose 102% year-on-year. Export value continues growing faster than volume: quantity is giving way to quality.

From dismantling the first foreign engine beside the Jialing River to the three-cylinder roar across the Jerez finish line, Chongqing has spent forty-seven years turning impossibilities into achievable tasks, one by one. As "a comprehensive inland opening-up hub" becomes tangible freight trains, customs declarations, and overseas agents' signatures, this landlocked city has proved that a place's reach is determined not by its distance from the sea, but by its closeness to the world.

The changes extend beyond selling motorcycles. On October 1, 2026, WorldSBK announced its 2027 calendar: Shanghai International Circuit would host for the first time, as the only Asian round and the season finale. Previously, China shipped motorcycles to the world; now the world brings its final to China's home ground. ZX Moto's two-and-a-half-year journey mirrors Chongqing's decades: connect the corridors, connect to the rules, then bring the world to your door.

ZX Moto's lessons extend far beyond one company's success or failure: personal breakthroughs depend on supply chains; technical breakthroughs on retaining control of core capabilities; innovative courage on an accommodating institutional environment; and expansive markets on open, efficient corridors.

One person runs in his chosen lane, aligned with his era. In China today, a complete industrial system provides rich soil for individual innovation, diverse demand opens space for specialization, and an improving institutional environment makes it possible to judge heroes without regard to origin. Those who believe "I can do it too" gain confidence to turn an idea into a business, together creating powerful momentum for the era's progress.

An individual runs fast through determination; a group travels far through corridors, rules, and fertile soil. ZX Moto's story invites reflection not because it proves that an individual can succeed, but because it shows how much a city, an industry, and an institutional system can raise an individual's chances of success.

Jerez's engines will eventually fall silent, but Chinese manufacturing's advancing footsteps grow clearer. That sound belongs to everyone who refuses to give up beneath a late-night desk lamp, and every ordinary person who believes "I can do it too." Together, these voices form this era's deepest narrative. (Contributing reporters and writers: Li Yuanyuan and Peng Bo)
