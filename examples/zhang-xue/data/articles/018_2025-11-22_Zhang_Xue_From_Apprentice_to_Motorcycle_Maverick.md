# Motorcycle Maverick Zhang Xue, from Huaihua, Hunan: From Repair Apprentice to China's Motorcycle Fanatic...

> Source: [Tencent News, "CEO Letters"](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20251122A04AYW00)

Thanks for waiting, everyone—the article I have been writing myself is finally ready!

They were Li Xiang, He Xiaopeng, Zhou Hongyi, Tim (Pan Tianhong), Ye Guofu, and He Tongxue.

Back to the point: today we are writing about another legendary figure.

Have you ever seen a vehicle-company boss encourage consumers to expose his own company online?

Have you ever seen a vehicle-company boss side with consumers and call after-sales service staff idiots?

Have you ever seen a vehicle-company boss deploy his company's legal team to help consumers defend their rights?

OK, today you will.

This boss works in motorcycles, rather than cars.

His name is Zhang Xue, and his company is Zhang Xue Motorcycles, or ZX Moto.

Some call him the Lei Jun of motorcycles, and I think that is fair. But after writing this piece, I feel he is more like the Steve Jobs of China's motorcycle industry. Will many people think that is excessive praise? Personally, I think it is all right!

In some ways, what Zhang does is even more impressive than what Lei Jun does. He may not personally open vehicle doors for users, but he uses ZX Moto's legal team to stand up for them—and has done so more than once.

Owners in the new-energy vehicle sector probably would not even dare imagine such a thing.

Good heavens! Look at how a motorcycle-company boss looks after his fans. New-energy vehicle owners, please stop getting so overwhelmed just because a billionaire boss bends down to open a door for you.

I knew little about Zhang Xue before. What prompted this profile was a news item I happened upon in late October: on October 19, the Kove team secured the WSBK World Superbike Championship's SSP300 overall season title at Spain's Jerez circuit.

It was the first overall season title for a Chinese motorcycle team in this premier world championship, ending the European, American, and Japanese brands' monopoly since the class was established nine years earlier.

Reportedly, Kove won against formidable opposition: 15 Japanese Kawasaki Ninja 400s, 12 Yamaha R3s, and three European KTM RC390s.

This also demonstrates that Chinese-made motorcycles offer outstanding performance at a world-class level.

I looked it up: the motorcycle industry has three world-leading road-racing competitions:

- The MotoGP World Championship, which holds a prestigious position in the industry and is regarded as "the F1 of road racing";

- The Superbike World Championship (WSBK), which uses modified production sportbikes and is called "the ultimate battleground for production motorcycles";

- The Isle of Man TT, known as "the most dangerous road race" because it takes place on public roads and is exceptionally dangerous, challenging, and spectacular.

Are we not talking about Zhang Xue? Why the sudden detour into Kove and motorcycle racing?

The reason is simple: Zhang Xue was also a founder of Kove.

By the time Kove won this year's title, however, Zhang had been gone from the company for more than a year. He has now founded Chongqing Zhangxue Motorcycle Industry Co., Ltd., known as "ZX Moto."

Let us take a minute to learn about ZX Moto founder Zhang Xue's upbringing and entrepreneurial journey:

In 1987, Zhang was born into a rural family in Tianjiawan Village, Jiangkouxu Town, Mayang County, Huaihua, Hunan Province.

In 1990, at age three, he experienced a major upheaval: his parents divorced. From then on, he grew up with his grandmother.

Around 1991, when he was four or five, he already loved motorcycles. As a child, he often used his clothing as a prop while pretending to ride one.

Zhang also remembered that, while he was in preschool, his mother suddenly returned to the village after years away and bought him a children's bicycle.

As an adult, Zhang wrote in his diary: "My mother came back from away and was pushing the bicycle home along the road when my teacher and I saw her. I had not seen her for years, but what I cared about then was actually the little bicycle she was pushing."

Zhang's wife, known online as "Sister Xing," shared his diary, "My Bond with Motorcycles."

Still an innocent child, Zhang was captivated by the little bicycle and did not understand that his parents' relationship had broken down.

In a diary entry titled "My Bond with Motorcycles," Zhang recalled his connection with motorcycles in detail. We will come to some of those details later.

At the end of the diary, he wrote "I love, love motorcycles" five times in succession. He wrote that neither high office nor great wealth could compare with being a rider. As long as he had once been a racer, he would willingly return home to farm afterward.

In plain language, Zhang expressed his devotion to motorcycles and his dream of racing. His handwriting may be somewhat untidy, but his passion leaps off the page.

Zhang's wife shared his diary notebook.

In 1995, eight-year-old Zhang began primary school in his hometown of Huaihua, Hunan.

According to Zhang, after the divorce, his father drifted around away from home, while his mother went to Fujian and married another man. What a poor child!

In 1997, ten-year-old Zhang and his younger sister lived independently in a dilapidated house. He said wind and rain came through the walls, and they could only cover the cracks with plastic sheeting.

This is the run-down house where Zhang lived as a child.

Many years later, Zhang took journalists back to that old house. One asked: "Did living in a shack like this not make you sad?"

Having endured so many hardships, Zhang showed no outward emotion at the question. He replied:

"When you have no choice, what good does being sad do? Besides, it was good for me: once you have tasted every kind of hardship, nothing later seems that bitter. Give you just a little sweetness, and you feel very happy."

In 1998, at eleven and in fourth grade, Zhang began riding a long-unused Phoenix bicycle after a village playmate damaged his children's bike.

He described his feelings in his diary: "My desire for a motorcycle was growing stronger and stronger."

Zhang was not an outstanding student. Someone online once shared a photograph supposedly showing his report card; the marks were indeed low, though I do not know whether it was authentic.

In 2001, fourteen-year-old Zhang started junior secondary school. Since the school was about ten li, roughly five kilometers, from home, his family bought him a mountain bike.

That year he also met the love of his life, "Sister Xing." From pursuing his racing dream penniless to starting a business from nothing in Chongqing, she stayed by his side. They have supported each other through hardship and enjoy a close relationship.

Sister Xing has shared the changes in Zhang's family circumstances and witnessed the changes in his life.

Between the second half of 2003 and the first half of 2004, while in his final year of junior secondary school, Zhang rode a motorcycle for the first time: a Jincheng Suzuki that a classmate had ridden to school. It took four attempts to get moving successfully, and his love of motorcycles grew every day.

Zhang recalled another episode in his diary. A red sportbike—a Suzuki Bandit—once came to Jiangkouxu Town. Its owner noticed him circling it for a long time and asked whether he liked it. Zhang said yes. The owner teased him: "If you like it, ride it away."

Zhang said he smiled and walked away, but inwardly told himself once again: "I must buy a sportbike."

In 2004, seventeen-year-old Zhang graduated from junior secondary school. He did not continue his education, instead becoming an apprentice at a Huaihua motorcycle shop to learn repairs.

He had set his heart on becoming a motorcycle racer early on, but without money he had to take an indirect route: become a shop apprentice, save enough for a motorcycle of his own, then look for a chance to race.

During his apprenticeship, Zhang gained close contact with motorcycles and a fuller understanding of large-displacement machines, generally those above 400cc.

Before long, he used RMB 8,000 in hard-earned savings to buy a "21-series V4." (That is how the model appears in his diary. My research suggests it was probably a Honda VFR400; please leave a correction if that is wrong.)

Zhang said the bike was in terrible condition. He replaced many parts, rode it for a while, then sold it and bought a "22-series V4."

During that period, his riding friends called him "Wild Man."

Zhang Xue, far left, with fellow riders in 2006.

Recalling himself at seventeen or eighteen, Zhang wrote: "I knew nothing about riding then. I thought whoever was fastest was a hero. I was always gambling with my life and had two really bad crashes. But I really did discover I was born to be a rider: the bike and the road barrier were smashed to pieces, while I suffered only minor injuries." (CEO Letters note: Please do not imitate this behavior.)

Zhang recently shared his early crash experiences.

Zhang reportedly also went to Anhui to learn to ride.

In late 2005 or early 2006, after a year and a half as an apprentice, he went independent and opened a small motorcycle shop in Mayang.

Zhang's motorcycle shop was also where his dream began.

He said he did not care much about income at the time. He would go riding at night, gradually moving away from "charging around like an idiot" and beginning to study racing techniques.

He often followed information about racer training, but still lacked the money for more professional instruction.

In 2006, nineteen-year-old Zhang felt that continuing this way would never make his racing dream come true, so he began seeking outside help.

He also knew how to attract attention: he repeatedly called Hunan Satellite TV's Evening program, recommending himself and offering to demonstrate motorcycle stunts on television.

In October 2006, after Zhang's relentless persistence, the crew decided to visit him in Mayang.

During the first shoot, however, rain had left roads and fields slippery. He failed to demonstrate his riding skills successfully, and the crew left disappointed.

Zhang did not lose heart. He sent the crew multimedia-message photographs and texts showing his practice.

He explained to the journalist: "I had never practiced in mud before. I am working hard to adapt. I am not afraid of hardship; I am afraid you will not give me a chance."

All he wanted was a chance to join a team through television exposure.

He wrote: "I know this pestering is embarrassing, and my riding friends laugh at my ideas as naive. But for my dream, I am willing to do it."

When the crew did not respond, Zhang rode a hundred-odd kilometers to their office the next day, hoping for a second shoot.

He told the television journalist: "I want you to film me again. I just want to join a motorcycle team. I am not trying to show off on television. I truly love motorcycles; I have loved them since childhood."

The journalist asked: "Is being on television that important to you?"

Zhang replied: "Being on television is not important. What matters is that a team sees me and lets me join."

Asked why, he explained: "Only by joining a team can you enter races—unless your family has several hundred thousand yuan to buy a bike, find a coach, and rent a track for training." His family had no money. His parents divorced when he was little, he grew up with his grandmother, and he and his sister had lived independently since he was ten.

At nineteen, Zhang rode a much-resold Honda that was twenty years old—older than he was.

The journalist asked what he would do if no team contacted him even after he appeared on television.

His answer left a deep impression on me: "Whether you succeed or fail, if you do not try when you are young, you will definitely regret it when you are old. If you try while you are young, even if you fail, you will have no regrets when you are old."

Though Zhang had little formal education, what he said at nineteen was deeply philosophical.

Despite his persistence, the Evening crew did not immediately agree to film again because the journalist had other assignments. Zhang continued to wear them down.

He told the journalist: "Even I think I am being shameless. Only I would do something like this. To put it nicely, it is persistence..."

The journalist asked: "And less nicely?"

Zhang said: "Less nicely, it is being a pest."

They exchanged a knowing smile.

Learning that the journalist was going to Mayang that day, Zhang decided to follow the crew back on his motorcycle. If they finished their interview early, perhaps there would be time to film him.

The crew drove ahead while Zhang followed on his motorcycle. Light rain began falling, the temperature was only about 10°C, and the three-hour journey involved slippery roads and heavy traffic.

He was eager to impress and wanted to show off his skills to the crew. Their driver thought his riding so dangerous that he even wanted to persuade him to turn back, but Zhang persisted.

Watching the broadcast footage of Zhang chasing the vehicle inspires both compassion and awe: compassion for his precarious circumstances, awe at his far-reaching ambition.

Honestly, even as a middle-aged man, I found this part unexpectedly moving and painful.

Eventually, Evening filmed his pursuit of his dream. After a crash in the video, Zhang said skills were learned through falling. When injured, he even produced his own bottle of Yunnan Baiyao medicine. He was clearly accustomed to crashing and getting hurt, and knew how to treat himself.

His riding might not have been polished or professional, but his desire to become a racer was passionate and sincere.

In just two days, even the journalists' attitude shifted from initial refusal to repeated encouragement.

Zhang told them that if he could join a team, he could endure any hardship. He was willing to repair motorcycles, cook, and wash clothes for the team—anything.

"Sooner or later, I will get a chance to race," he said with conviction.

He had also considered failure: "If I do not succeed, I will work like mad to make money. If I have a son, I will make him race even if he does not like it."

At the end of the Hunan Satellite TV program, Zhang rode away, looking back and shouting: "If you have a dream, go after it! Courage makes my life more exciting!"

Zhang's childhood was unhappy: a broken family, a lack of parental love, and venturing alone into society as a teenager. Remarkably, he acquired no bad habits. He possessed the rare combination of an ability to heal himself, resilience through repeated failure, and the courage to pursue his dream.

Heaven truly helps those who help themselves! His story brings to mind the line: "Why be surprised if the world does not know you? The hero acts resolutely, regardless."

Remember this face that never admits defeat.

After Zhang's story aired on Hunan Satellite TV in early 2007, many people contacted and encouraged him. Several teams said they would take him.

But he wrote in his diary that after he closed his shop and prepared to join, all those supposedly willing teams became unreachable. They seemed more like fraudsters.

There was just one person, a former motorcycle racer from a People's Liberation Army unit in Hainan who had since entered business. He explained the requirements for being a racer, and Zhang learned that he was already rather old and somewhat small in stature.

The former racer also gave Zhang the number of an old teammate who was now coaching in Beijing. Zhang happily went to visit him.

In Beijing, the coach taught Zhang correct riding positions, made training plans, and promised to let him enter the 2007 "JL Cup." (Zhang's handwriting is untidy, so that competition name may not be accurate.) Whether the team would take him depended on his results.

Zhang later wrote in his diary:

"I am already very content and happy to have this opportunity. My shop is closed, all my money has gone into a YZ [probably a motorcycle], and I have no income. That YZ drinks so much fuel, but I am still very happy.

"Motorcycle racing draws me so strongly that I will leave a pot on the fire unattended to watch a race on television. I will give up everything else to enter motorcycle sport. To buy the bike more fuel, I never buy good things for myself. I love, love motorcycles..."

This diary entry was probably written during the years when Zhang was learning to race.

In April 2007, twenty-year-old Zhang took RMB 60,000 in savings to join a team in Siyang, Jiangsu. He still remembered seeing motorcycles flying around a racing circuit for the first time: the excitement was beyond words.

Based on the information I found, the team was probably the "Yu'an Shock Absorber Team," affiliated with the Yu'an Group.

The team did not initially train him, but a professional racer named Zhang Jixing, nicknamed "Brother Ya," was willing to teach him.

That is how Zhang stayed with the team as a stunt rider and mechanic.

Many people therefore consider Zhang Jixing one of Zhang Xue's first mentors.

"Brother Ya" is quite a legendary character himself. After winning multiple motocross championship rounds, he developed an interest in extreme sports, earned a pilot's license, and learned to fly aircraft.

In recent years, he invested RMB 30 million in an extreme-sports facility called Brother Ya's Outdoor Sports Camp. In December 2024, he announced that he would stop updating his personal account with over 1.8 million followers and instead build high-performance aircraft. I will leave it there; interested readers can look him up.

In 2009, twenty-two-year-old Zhang could no longer sustain his racing studies after a long period without income.

He joined Zhejiang Apollo Motorcycle Company, working on product development and riding for Apollo's off-road team.

It is also worth mentioning Apollo's owner, Ying Er, another interesting person: the world's only female entrepreneur manufacturing off-road motorcycles.

Shortly after joining Apollo, Zhang planned an ambitious project: riding a motorcycle across water.

In 2009, CCTV-10's People and Society covered his attempt to make a motorcycle skim across water.

The program included his five attempts to climb a roadside dirt slope. He succeeded only on the fifth try, vividly displaying the struggle, frustration, urgency, persistence, and eventual success.

This small incident also reveals Zhang's tenacity: he never retreats from difficulty.

The program also showed him assembling an engine blindfolded. In one or two hours, he successfully assembled a motorcycle engine, explaining that he had dismantled so many that he knew their details inside out.

It also showed the outcome of his water-riding attempt. He simply welded two flat plates alongside the front and rear wheels, hoping momentum would allow the motorcycle to skim some distance across water. He suffered abrasions around his waist and abdomen on the first attempt but insisted on trying again.

Unfortunately, neither of his two attempts reached 25 meters. At most, he covered about 20 meters.

In May 2015, Australian world-class freestyle motocross rider Robbie Maddison rode his KTM 250 SX off-road motorcycle across the water off Tahiti.

Comparing the videos reveals that the foreign rider's motorcycle design followed much the same idea as Zhang's rudimentary apparatus. Zhang had tried it six years earlier.

Reports also said the overseas rider prepared for three years before successfully "riding on water."

Left: Zhang's motorcycle at the time. Right: the overseas rider's motorcycle.

An aside: that foreign motorcycle rider also worked as a stunt performer in the 2012 James Bond film Skyfall.

In 2011, Zhang represented Apollo's off-road team in a race in Linfen. Someone posted photographs in 2023, saying Zhang was actually the entire team's only rider.

Zhang said he worked at Apollo's off-road motorcycle company for about four years altogether.

There are also rumors that he parted ways with his former boss at twenty-five over differences in product philosophy, as mentioned in the image below.

If 2006–2009 was his period of pursuing a rider's dream, then 2009–2012 marked a gradual transition from rider to product developer. His repair skills began laying the foundations for his future motorcycle-building career.

In early 2013, twenty-six-year-old Zhang arrived in Chongqing with his entire savings of RMB 20,000. He initially settled in the Konggang Shengdi residential community. He had no resources and knew only that Chongqing's more mature motorcycle supply chain made it a suitable place to build bikes.

He found a comprehensive motorcycle-parts market in Chongqing, spent his entire RMB 20,000 on components, and assembled a motorcycle according to his own ideas.

As he assembled it, he posted on online forums including Motobar, Motomimi, and MotoYes, attracting attention and support from many enthusiasts.

He was already something of an online personality in motorcycle circles, so forum members were willing to trust him. He sold the bike successfully, then used the proceeds to assemble better ones.

In the second half of 2013, Zhang, aged twenty-seven, joined the Chongqing Huanghe Motorcycles team and subsequently helped develop models including the Huanghe Freedom 250X and Freedom 300X.

I specifically searched motorcycle forums for posts about him. The specifications of the new motorcycle Zhang launched in 2014 followed a value-for-money strategy: comparable equipment might cost RMB 28,000 from other brands, while their bike was around RMB 13,000–15,000.

Below are online comments from 2014 about the Freedom 300X. Judging from the discussions, its specifications did offer a value advantage.

The Huanghe Freedom series ultimately achieved only limited success, however, and attracted criticism over product quality and after-sales service.

Even in 2019, someone wrote on a forum: "Because of it, I learned to repair motorcycles. Even mechanics cannot source the parts." The accompanying photo appears to show the Huanghe Freedom 300X Zhang and his colleagues developed. (Please correct me if I am wrong; I do not know motorcycles deeply.)

More than one person said things like: "You have to hunt down parts and modify them. You go from novice to repair expert."

Of course, these experiences and lessons made Zhang place greater importance on product quality and dependable after-sales service later.

In April 2016, twenty-nine-year-old Zhang established Chongqing Freedom Motorcycle Sales Co., Ltd. and organized a community called the "Freedom Riders Alliance." The company has since been deregistered.

In 2017, thirty-year-old Zhang co-founded Tibet Kove Industrial Co., Ltd. with Liao Tao and Wen Famo. This later became Kove Motorcycles.

He served as general manager and held 37.05% of the company, making him its largest individual shareholder.

He also established Kove's product philosophy: performance first! In every class, pursue the ultimate in light weight, high power, and top-level handling.

In August 2018, Zhang led Kove in launching its first production motorcycle, the Kove 500X, priced at RMB 32,800 for the standard version and RMB 37,800 for the adventure version.

Positioned as a rugged middleweight ADV, the 500X put Kove on the map and pioneered lightweight Chinese ADV motorcycles. (PS: ADV means adventure motorcycle.)

When the Kove 500X launched, the only comparable ADV available domestically was the foreign-brand Benelli 502X. Kove quickly captured market share through positioning that offered far better value than imported brands.

Zhang later disclosed that they sold 800 motorcycles after the 2018 launch, rising to 3,000 in 2019, with continued growth afterward.

In April 2019, he led the launch of the youth-oriented Kove 400X, priced below RMB 30,000.

In August 2019, thirty-two-year-old Zhang wanted to develop a motorcycle engine in-house. His reasoning was that without its own engine, a motorcycle company would inevitably face a critical dependency in the future.

Kove's partners disagreed with developing engines independently, perhaps concerned about the large investment and high risk.

Zhang offered two options: he would either walk away with nothing and develop engines himself, or borrow company money for the project. He promised: "If it succeeds, the engine belongs to the company; if it fails, the debt is mine."

Thus, as Kove's boss, Zhang borrowed RMB 10 million from the company to begin independently developing an 800cc liquid-cooled parallel-twin engine, code-named EBS01. Kove later invested more than another RMB 20 million in the engine project.

He did not disappoint his partners. After a year and a half of development, on February 5, 2021, his team's independently developed 800cc liquid-cooled parallel twin fired up successfully.

This ended Chinese large-displacement engines' long dependence on "reverse engineering," meaning dismantling and imitating existing products. Zhang livestreamed the milestone.

Meanwhile, in September 2020, at thirty-three, he initiated a 400cc four-cylinder engine-development project, code-named Z459MQ-A.

At the time, imported brands almost monopolized China's middleweight four-cylinder supersport market, generally priced above RMB 100,000. Their independently developed four-cylinder engine targeted the RMB 30,000–40,000 segment.

Together, the planned 400cc inline-four and the previously initiated 800cc liquid-cooled twin created a complementary lower- and higher-end product lineup for Kove.

In December 2021, Zhang led Kove in introducing the 525, an upgrade of the 500X, priced at RMB 28,500.

On January 26, 2022, the team's independently developed 400cc-class four-cylinder engine also fired up successfully, making Kove the first Chinese brand to take an engine to 16,000rpm.

The successful firing of these two in-house engines also marked a turning point for China's motorcycle industry, from "assembly and contract manufacturing" to "technological innovation."

In the following period, Kove's 800X series became "China's cheapest hardcore medium-to-large-displacement ADV," while the 450RR series broke through the RMB 100,000-plus price barrier of imported four-cylinder supersports, becoming "the only four-cylinder supersport in the RMB 30,000 class."

Zhang's precise engine positioning and foresight identified gaps in the market, quickly establishing Kove while giving ordinary consumers higher-performance motorcycles at lower prices. How similar this is to Xiaomi's idea of "making high-end products accessible to everyone."

And all this was achieved by a team led by Zhang, who had only a junior secondary education.

When Cheng Qian asked, "You graduated from junior secondary school and learned motorcycle repair. How did you manage to develop engines?"

Zhang's answer was simple: "If you do not know how, learn!"

When suppliers were reluctant to cooperate, he patiently explained that successful development would bring greater returns to both sides, eventually winning their support.

During the difficult early days of his business, Zhang borrowed money from some fellow riders. Once the company had established itself, in 2022 he repaid their kindness by giving each a half-price Kove voucher marked "No restrictions on model or expiry date."

In September 2022, at a launch in Ningxia's Tengger Desert, Zhang announced the Kove 450 Rally.

In October 2022, at the Chongqing Motorcycle Expo, he unveiled the Kove 400RR, the first motorcycle fitted with Kove's in-house 400cc four-cylinder engine. It was shown only as a prototype and never entered production.

By then, Zhang was arranging Kove's product lineup like an experienced, wise strategist deploying troops. With money and capability behind him, he also resumed pursuing his dream!

He had set Kove the goal of "entering top-level competitions and achieving results." From late 2022 to early 2023, thirty-five-year-old Zhang led the team into the premier off-road event, the Dakar Rally.

It was Kove's first Dakar entry as a Chinese manufacturer. Kove became the first Chinese motorcycle manufacturer to finish the Dakar Rally and the first manufacturer worldwide to have every motorcycle finish on its debut, opening a new chapter for Chinese entrants.

Zhang also drew up a Dakar victory plan:

- Enter in 2023 to gain experience

- Finish in the top 20 in 2024

- Challenge for the top ten in 2025

- Collect a trophy in 2026

- Win in 2027!

On May 19, 2023, the Kove 800X officially launched as the first motorcycle with Kove's independently developed 800cc liquid-cooled parallel-twin engine.

Kove also officially introduced the 450RR, an upgrade of the 400RR, positioned as "Kove's first four-cylinder sportbike" and "China's first small-to-medium-displacement four-cylinder model."

That same month, thirty-six-year-old Zhang led Kove's first entry in China's Taklimakan Rally, also competing personally as a rider. The team won five stages.

He briefly lost consciousness after a crash during the race. Upon waking, he found he had a broken finger and other injuries. Despite teammates' objections, he insisted on racing that day and the next, earning widespread praise for his spirit.

He said he initially had no idea how the crash had knocked him out. Only after riding some distance did he remember being clipped by a car overtaking him.

Zhang was knocked unconscious in the Taklimakan Rally and continued racing injured.

His plan for Kove's first year at the Taklimakan Rally was "finish and learn." For the second year, the target was "win and establish dominance."

In early 2024, he also began taking the Kove team through the procedures to enter the top-tier international WSBK competition, the Superbike World Championship.

Unfortunately, he did not get to lead Kove into the Taklimakan Rally or international competition again.

On March 1, 2024, he suddenly announced on WeChat Moments that he had resigned from Kove, with no next job lined up.

He posted his resignation letter:

"After careful consideration, I have decided to resign to pursue my stars and seas. In the future, we will be friends, and also competitors. Until we meet again."

It was dated February 28, 2024.

The next day, he said he had focused only on his dream and neglected investors' returns, also indicating that differences in business philosophy with investors lay behind his departure.

Zhang announces his resignation on WeChat Moments.

He later described leaving the Kove business he had helped build as one of the major setbacks of his life.

It reminded me of Steve Jobs being forced out of Apple.

Zhang disclosed that before leaving, the company had asked him to step down as general manager. He had accepted this and agreed to remain only as vice president of R&D.

But when he wrote a lengthy proposal urging continued R&D investment in new engines, the company rejected it.

On innovation in motorcycles, he once said: "As long as you push forward, you can break through. But if you do not push, death is only a matter of time."

He felt there was no point staying if new engines would not be developed. Perhaps he could stay and collect money without much effort, but money was not what he wanted. He wanted to do what he believed was right and worthwhile.

So he decisively resigned from Kove.

In April 2024, thirty-seven-year-old Zhang established a motorcycle brand bearing his own name: ZX Moto.

He set his new company the goal of becoming one of China's top three within three years and one of the world's top ten within ten years.

In May–June 2024, two months after his departure, the Kove team competed in the Taklimakan Rally. Riding the 450 Rally series he had developed, its riders swept all eight stages, becoming the first Chinese team to achieve that distinction.

In July 2024, Zhang secured angel investment for ZX Moto from Gaoxin Capital. He disclosed that he currently owned 80% of the company and the angel investor 20%.

I found that Gaoxin Capital had previously invested in chip company New Vision Microelectronics, AI company Geek+, and aerospace company Galactic Energy. Most of its portfolio companies belong to the "hard technology" sector.

In September 2024, Zhang's new company unveiled its first motorcycle, the 500RR, during the Chongqing Motorcycle Expo.

In October 2024, Kove's independently developed 350RR won a race at the final round of the 2024 WSBK season in Jerez, Spain.

This was Kove's first international race victory and a historic global competition win for a Chinese motorcycle manufacturer. China's national anthem rang out at the circuit!

The five-star red flag flew at an international WSBK venue!

Though Zhang had already left Kove, when he heard the news after work, he wept in a taxi.

He said: "The national anthem will be played more often from now on. The path is open, and people will believe in us more and more."

Behind Kove's race win stood the in-house engine development Zhang had championed, helping Chinese manufacturers escape the embarrassing "Dipingxian" label. (In motorcycle circles, "Dipingxian," literally "horizon," is a collective term for imitation sportbikes assembled by small Chinese factories or workshops.)

A year later, in October 2025, Kove won the WSBK overall season title, further vindicating Zhang's foresight.

In December 2024, a young Guangdong man using the handle "Xiaomu Ah" challenged himself to persuade a manufacturer to sponsor a motorcycle through his own efforts alone. Drawn by Zhang's reputation, he ultimately received Zhang's readily offered sponsorship and set off around China on a ZX Moto. By November 7, 2025, the bike had carried him 12,000 kilometers.

Of course, Zhang has also made mistakes as an entrepreneur.

Closing thoughts:

Some say his life story closely resembles that of Honda founder Soichiro Honda.

I looked into Honda's life and found quite a few striking parallels with this "father of Japanese motorcycles."

Both rose from humble origins. Honda, a blacksmith's son, had only a primary education; Zhang had only a junior secondary education and opened a repair shop at seventeen. Both were apprentices.

Both were also technology obsessives. Honda rarely dwelt on transactional gains and losses, putting innovation and invention first. Likewise, Zhang left his previous company without another job to develop new engines. He also opened the door to independent engine development in China's motorcycle industry, allowing more consumers to ride high-performance machines.

Both named their brands after themselves: one Honda, the other Zhang Xue.

Just as Soichiro Honda profoundly influenced Japan's motorcycle industry, Zhang has already rewritten China's motorcycle history. Because of him, Chinese motorcycles have delivered more exciting performances internationally.

From the nineteen-year-old pursuing his dream through cold rain on a Hunan road to the founder of a new brand establishing Chinese motorcycles' standing internationally, Zhang's nearly twenty years of striving offer a passionate illustration of dreams becoming reality.

His life reads like an epic of an ordinary person's rise, demonstrating that the starting point never defines the finish. Only a heart that refuses defeat and burns with dedication, together with courage strengthened by setbacks and the action of "ten times the effort," can carry someone through every low point and every doubt to live their own legend.

The road ahead remains long. But Zhang and his motorcycles, just as he yearned for in youth, are heading faster than ever toward the wider world of stars and seas that belongs to him!
