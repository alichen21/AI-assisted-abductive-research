## Zhang Xue: One Man, and the Many Behind Him

Staff reporters Li Xinping, Jiang Feng, Chang Qin, and Wang Xinyue

May 12, 2026, 08:46 | Source: People.cn–People's Daily | 222

Subscribe | Unsubscribe | Saved | Save | Larger text

Click to hear this article, approximately

May 5 marked the Start of Summer.

Over this spring, ZX Moto went from obscurity to sudden nationwide attention.

Looking back, Unitree emerged in 2024, DeepSeek burst onto the scene in 2025, and ZX Moto made its name in 2026... It seems every spring brings new stars into the sky.

Who are they? What qualities do they share? How do they break through?

Who is Zhang Xue?

What can happen in four seconds?

One deep breath, six to eight blinks, fifteen spoken Chinese characters. In everyday life, four seconds pass in an instant.

But in the Superbike World Championship (WSBK), four seconds cover 200 meters, almost half a straight. In a sport decided by tenths of a second, that is an enormous gap.

March 28, local time, Portimão circuit, Portugal: the checkered flag waved as ZX Moto crossed the finish line nearly four seconds ahead! It was the first race win by a Chinese motorcycle in WSBK's middleweight class.

Yet just over twenty days later came a sharp reversal.

At the Netherlands round in April: fourth in Race 1, seventh in Race 2. At Hungary in May: a Race 1 victory, then retirement in Race 2.

Zhang responded calmly and rationally: "Nobody can keep winning forever. Everyone faces failure."

Facing and handling failure has become a central theme in his more than twenty years of life in the fast lane.

ZX Moto was registered in 2024. Putting his personal name on the brand, he said, "means putting myself on the line."

He invested all his savings and even borrowed widely: "Even the wage money was borrowed. If the new bike could not launch within three months, the company's cash flow would break."

Yet this cash-strapped brand announced that it would spend heavily to enter world-class motorcycle racing.

"We make supersport motorcycles. If we do it, we must make the best and prove ourselves at the highest-level events," he answered directly.

But what if it failed? Or if success on the track did not translate into business success?

He calculated it this way: "If I cannot earn it back, I will consider it paying for my hobby. Others may like paintings or travel. I like nothing else—only this."

That passion underpins every choice he makes.

More than thirty years ago, Zhang was born in a remote mountain village in Huaihua, Hunan, a world away from WSBK.

At nineteen, hearing that a Hunan Satellite TV crew was filming motorcycle enthusiasts, he climbed onto his old secondhand motorcycle and chased them for over 100 kilometers through heavy rain.

The mountain road was rough, rain unrelenting, water beating his face. After three hours he was soaked, his lips bluish, but he refused to stop.

"Go home. It is too dangerous," the crew urged.

"You do not need to persuade me. I am not afraid of hardship; I am afraid you will not give me a chance."

He wanted only "a chance to take part." For that chance, he threw himself into production work.

At Zhejiang Wuyi Apollo Sports Technology Co., Ltd., he developed an astonishing skill: assembling engines blindfolded. Using only touch and muscle memory, he could restore scattered components into a precise power unit.

After founding his business, he repeatedly told his technical team: "Not enough people? Hire! Equipment inadequate? Buy! If you cannot solve it, I will find outside experts and consultants. But the problem must be solved."

At ZX Moto, frontline assembly workers earn hourly wages 20% above comparable local businesses. Motorcycle testers are provided with camper vans so they can better test performance in uninhabited areas.

"We may be poor, but I am willing to pay for innovation," he said.

Portugal made him famous overnight, bringing twenty-plus years of accumulation into public view and making him an outstanding representative of ordinary entrepreneurs.

"I do not deserve that. I am not perfect," he told the reporter.

In his office, a palm-sized desk sign reads "Do not shout," a gift bought especially by colleagues.

He makes unannounced rounds, suddenly appearing behind a workstation and silently watching the screen. Sometimes a suddenly raised voice stuns the whole office. "Most colleagues who work with me have been hurt to some degree. I never soften my words," he fully acknowledges.

But after the temper passes, his rule is firm: "Go do it. If you succeed, the credit is yours. If you fail, I bear the cost."

The same applies to suppliers. "My demanding requirements are enormous challenges. They must overturn existing technology, try repeatedly, experiment, and may fail," he said. "But I tell them not to worry: I bear all costs. Once it succeeds, they keep the added value."

"Entrepreneurship is hard, so choose something you love. Only passion helps you withstand all kinds of shocks," he sincerely advises young people.

Why Zhang Xue?

Why could Zhang succeed?

Travel through Chongqing and meet the people behind him, and the answer becomes clear.

Climbing the stairs inside Qiutian Gear's office building, sunlight falls on old photographs, and time seems to stretch out—

Founder Fu Zhongqiu is seventy-five, his hands still strong as iron pincers. In 1993, the former Chongqing medical-equipment factory fitter staked his RMB 10,000 savings, rented two discarded machines—a grinding machine and a gear hobber—and started a business with three bold workers.

Sixteen years later, Qiutian Gear became China's largest motorcycle-gear producer. Today, one in every five motorcycles worldwide uses its products.

How did it succeed?

"No other way: early starts, late finishes, sleeping in the workshop." Fu distilled his experience into three principles, engraved atop the company's honors wall: dedication, focus, professionalism.

How specialized is it? Qiutian has consistently concentrated on small- and medium-module gears. Its gears for high-end large-displacement motorcycles run at up to 30,000rpm with micron-level precision.

In 2023, a Fortune Global 500 company issued a call for a new gear design for a product launch. The short development period deterred peers, but Qiutian accepted. Fu's team overcame the technical challenge in just twenty-seven days, completing everything from innovation to sample delivery.

Recently, a Japanese company requested a special gear structure that local suppliers could not produce. Qiutian accepted again. Japanese peers called it "impossible" and sent people to Chongqing to watch the workshop for three weeks. They left convinced. "Now, when they face difficult problems, they ask us," Fu said calmly.

If an engine is a motorcycle's heart, gears are its valves, while the cylinder head is its ventricle, where the four strokes—intake, compression, power, exhaust—are completed.

Below Zhongliang Mountain sits another hidden industry champion and ZX Moto supplier: Chongqing Zhicheng Machinery. It makes more than 15 million cylinder heads annually, holding 70% of China's market and 25% globally.

"We used to do only machining. Now international major brands invite us to help design new products," said general manager Deng Wanqing, raising his eyebrows. This participation in upstream design reflects global supply chains' trust in the company's ability to turn processes into reality.

Nearby on the Jialing River is high-tech company Yujiang Lanfeng Power, which makes the engine's "atrium," the crankcase. General manager Sun Likai said: "Molds are the key." Improved mold design, development, and manufacturing cut new-product development from ninety days to sixty and generated hundreds of core patents.

Cylinder pistons as "heart muscle," oil passages as "blood vessels," electronic components as "nerves"... A motorcycle has thousands of parts, and behind Zhang stand thousands upon thousands of entrepreneurs. They may never appear in the spotlight, but they are crucial support for his progress.

Looking beyond the depth of the supply chain to the breadth of the market, Zhang has many companions too.

Chongqing's Loncin is another example of Chinese-made motorcycles going global. Yin Bing, born in the 1980s, has worked there for twenty years and became general manager of its import-export subsidiary this March.

Graduating in 2006, Yin had two offers: procurement at a joint venture paying 50% more, or foreign trade at local private company Loncin. After much thought, he chose the latter: "I believed Chinese-made products could go global."

In 2018, Loncin launched its premium Voge brand. Entering Europe posed a difficult choice. Yin overruled objections: "If we are building a premium brand, we must tackle the hardest challenge. Otherwise, we are only playing on a small scale."

But the first-generation touring motorcycle met a cool European reception. "Salespeople fight on the front line; they can hear the gunfire." Yin rapidly relayed customer needs and worked with R&D to adjust the product: "Then we produced three hits in a row!"

Last year, Voge exported over 100,000 motorcycles to Europe, with overseas revenue exceeding domestic revenue for the first time, securing a global-market bridgehead. In this year's first quarter, European export value grew 30%. "This is no stroke of luck; the brand has built momentum," he said.

From Fu sleeping in the workshop to Yin securing a bridgehead, a clear pattern emerges.

"I grew by standing on giants' shoulders. Even if I had not won this time, a Wang Xue or Li Xue would certainly have won for Chinese motorcycles," Zhang said.

His case shows that in China, the distance between an idea and manufacturing may be shorter than anywhere else. A drawing for a nonstandard component can become a mold and sample without leaving an industrial park. A new product can undergo testing day and night on established test benches. A complete industrial system continually lowers innovation costs.

Together, the supply chain's hidden champions tell a story of collective effort and how an era supports an individual.

Where is the next "Zhang Xue"?

ZX Moto's racing continues, but a deeper question has emerged: what industrial soil and institutional environment turn dreams into reality? Where is the next Zhang Xue?

In 2025, the emergence of Hangzhou's "Six Little Dragons" made its service philosophy, "Do not disturb unless necessary," widely known. How do needs differ when the businesses served are manufacturers rather than technology companies?

Liang Xiao is deputy director of Chongqing Liangjiang New Area's Economy and Information Technology Commission. In November 2025, Chongqing adjusted its administrative divisions, abolishing Jiangbei and Yubei districts and establishing Liangjiang New Area.

After the restructuring, he noticed ZX Moto in handover documents. A former Yubei colleague's comment—"This one has potential"—stayed with him.

In February 2026, he visited with his team and identified the company's central need, capacity expansion, in under half an hour. That evening, a list of land, financing, and talent requirements reached district leaders' desks.

On the third day, the responsible district leader met Zhang face to face. A local state-owned platform company attended, and land supply and cooperation terms were settled item by item on the spot. From initial agreement to finalized contract took less than two months.

Land alone was insufficient. Liang and colleagues brought in the district financial office to connect banks and tailor financing, then the science and technology bureau and intellectual property bureau for one-to-one help with unicorn-company applications, patents, product certification, and more.

Liang has over 200 businesses of ZX Moto's scale in his phone contacts. Their difficulties are often seemingly small: a turnover-based loan not approved, paperwork for a few mu of land delayed, or long waits for certification reports.

Asked the most important skill for officials serving industry, he thought for a long time, then said: "Being able to respond. When a business speaks, you understand. When it faces a problem, you help find the person who can solve it."

Turning dreams into reality needs both government support and investors who recognize potential.

For ZX Moto, that investor was Gaoxin Capital founding partner Cao Bin. Just three months after ZX Moto was founded, Gaoxin exclusively provided RMB 20 million in angel investment.

Asked why he invested in an unfashionable traditional industry, Cao said: "We backed the person. We look for four founder qualities: passion, focus, resilience, and expertise."

Nationwide, after decades of development, Chinese venture capital is gradually maturing into patient capital that accompanies businesses' growth. At the end of February 2026, the combined size of venture-capital and private-equity funds reached RMB 14.96 trillion.

State-owned capital plays a very important role.

Hao Yiyang heads Investment Department I at Chengtong's science and technology innovation investment fund under China Chengtong. In April, each team member investigated an average of ten companies. The fund has a fifteen-year term and a clear objective: invest small, early, and in hard technology.

"Compared with market-oriented institutions, state-owned funds have a narrower sector focus and more patience," he explained. They seek more often to provide help when needed, like Zhejiang state capital's support for ZX Moto. "We hope to discover more Hangzhou 'Six Little Dragons.'"

At Chengtong, investment in a promising business can take as little as two and a half months from initiation. Despite the speed, business, financial, and legal due diligence is extensive, covering upstream and downstream companies as well as competitors.

After investing, investors also support companies comprehensively, from sharing information and matching needs to financing plans.

"Chengtong uses a co-investment mechanism. Every investor must put personal money into chosen businesses," Hao said with a smile. "Every project is our child."

When patient capital that values industry's future over short-term profit becomes mainstream, dreams becoming reality will no longer be isolated cases.

Perhaps this is the secret of Chinese striving: society as a whole opens pathways for those who do practical work. A society that judges heroes without regard to their origins draws on deep industrial foundations to support ordinary dreamers, making the sparks of innovation burn brighter.

(Staff reporter Wang Yinxin also contributed reporting and writing.)
