## The Zhang Xue Legend: How a Rural Mechanic Outran Century-Old Giants

Economic Observer | Follow | 2026-03-31 16:10

Economic Observer reporters Wang Yajie and Zhang Xiaohui

March 28, 2026. Portimão circuit, Algarve, Portugal.

At the Portuguese round of the Superbike World Championship (WSBK), Chinese manufacturer ZX Moto's number 53 machine won Race 1 in the WorldSSP class.

The five-star red flag rose above the podium, marking Chinese manufacturing's first victory in this event.

Chinese motorcycle manufacturer ZX Moto's number 53 won the first WorldSSP race at WSBK's Portuguese round by 3.685 seconds.

On March 29, Valentin Debise rode number 53 to another victory in Race 2, becoming a double winner.

Behind ZX Moto lay a string of names with deep histories in global motorcycling: Kawasaki Heavy Industries, founded in 1878; Triumph in 1902; BMW in 1921; Honda in 1948; and Ducati in 1953.

However, this WorldSSP middleweight victory surpassed those brands' performances in that class and those races; it did not represent comprehensive superiority over their overall technological capabilities.

Watching the live broadcast at headquarters in distant Chongqing, founder Zhang Xue leaped up, waved the national flag, and shouted into the camera: "We won!" Then he crouched down, tears streaming.

He was crying again. This man from Hunan is always crying.

He had cried in a car until the driver thought something was "wrong with him"; cried before a screen watching the Kove brand he helped create race while he was no longer part of it; cried with his face covered, crouching on the ground, or holding a microphone. Every time, it involved motorcycles. Every tear marked a fragment of a dream being fitted back into place.

This dreamer from rural Hunan, with only a junior secondary education, spent twenty years engraving his determination into motorcycles' steel bodies.

On the afternoon of March 30, the "motorcycle fanatic" was surrounded by Economic Observer and other media at ZX Moto's Chongqing headquarters.

Emotional, persistent, candid, even a little rough-hewn: this is Zhang Xue. Behind him stands a Chinese motorcycle newcomer valued at RMB 1.09 billion, racing toward the global industry's top ten. Beneath his feet is a legendary path from "impossible" to "achieved," paved with "ten times the effort."

Shouts and tears at Portimão

Rewind two days to Portugal. When number 53 crossed the finish line, ZX Moto's pit erupted. The significance went far beyond a trophy.

In WSBK, the world's highest-level production-motorcycle competition, the middleweight class has long been an arena for manufacturers' technical prowess. Its relatively open rules make it a crucial stepping stone to the premier class. For decades, giants including Honda, Yamaha, Kawasaki, and Ducati have all but monopolized its podium.

"Why did we win? Fundamentally, our motorcycle is competitive," Zhang explained on March 30, analyzing the details fluently. "For the same displacement, we have the most horsepower, lowest weight, and lowest chassis center of gravity. Our racing bike is also more modular than the road version, with a wider adjustment range. In wet races, for example, changing the anti-squat angle improves the rear wheel's contribution under braking. Trail is adjustable: greater for stability at large circuits, smaller for agility at small ones. Our engineers, including me, refined all this bit by bit."

A few years earlier, speaking to business-interview channel and independent media account Cheng Qian's Circle of Friends, he delivered a forceful line later widely quoted: "Perhaps I am inferior to you in every other way—less talent, fewer resources. But when I work ten times harder, why should it not be mine? It is mine!"

Those words received their loudest answer at Portimão. The Hunan teenager who once chased television journalists for three hours on an eighteenth-hand motorcycle announced to the world through a resounding victory: a Chinese self-taught engineer with a junior secondary education and a grassroots team had wrested a gold medal from the global motorcycle giants through ten times the usual effort.

One media commentator compared it to Zhang Weili winning a UFC championship belt: a breakthrough of exceptional significance. A professional racer told Economic Observer it was like China's men's football team reaching the World Cup final and lifting the trophy. Jokes aside, the victory truly opened a shaft of light for China's motorcycle industry.

On March 30, Zhang said: "My twenty years in this industry effectively equal forty for someone else. I take only five full days off out of 365. Apart from sleeping and eating, I spend almost every moment thinking about motorcycles. I have worked deeply across the whole supply chain. I know how to build a good bike. I understand enough, have enough expertise, and work hard enough. I can definitely do it well."

Twenty years of following his own course

Rewind to a rainy day in Hunan in 2006. A teenager on a battered motorcycle followed a television interview vehicle along muddy roads for three hours. When it stopped, he stood before the journalist, soaked through, and said: "I want to show you my riding skills."

That teenager was nineteen-year-old Zhang. He had recently finished junior secondary school and was learning motorcycle repairs in his hometown. He dreamed of racing, but dreams need money. After several years competing, he had spent all his savings with unremarkable results and had to face reality: he first needed to support himself.

"I could do nothing except motorcycle-related work. I did not want another industry either; I still wanted to stay in this one," he recalled.

He went to work at a Zhejiang motorcycle factory. In the workshop, he worked through almost the entire process: reverse engineering, procurement, quality inspection, manufacturing, and after-sales service.

During those years, he witnessed the unruly growth of Chinese motorcycles from imitation to an initial rise, while keenly feeling the gap from the world's best. His racing dream revived: if he could not become the best rider, he would build a motorcycle capable of beating world champions.

In 2014, he co-founded Kove with partners. He led development of several rally models, whose performance and value quickly built a reputation at home and abroad. But disagreements arose.

"I proposed a three-cylinder engine project, and it was rejected. On the surface, the reason was insufficient money, but really I had no authority," he later said about the split. "When even my suggestions were not accepted, why agonize? So I stopped."

In 2024, he chose to walk away with nothing. That night, drinking with friends, he cried again. Kove contained his heart and soul, but he had to leave to do what he truly wanted.

He then founded ZX Moto, bearing his own name. "In my view, however good a name sounds, a brand cannot be high-end if its products are low-end. When the product is good enough, any name will do—even Zhang Xue," he said.

In 2024, seeing Kove achieve a WSBK breakthrough, he cried in a car for so long that his fringe stuck to his sweaty forehead. In an independent-media interview, he said even the driver thought something was "wrong with him."

He knew then that the motorcycle-building direction he had championed was right. But the pain of having that validated was harder to bear than failure.

He wiped away his tears and entered ZX Moto's workshop to build a racing machine truly representing his spirit.

From muddy Hunan paths to Zhejiang production lines, Chongqing's R&D center, and finally the summit of world motorcycle competition in Portugal, the journey took twenty years. "Whether you succeed or fail, if you do not try when young, you will regret it when old. If you try while young, even if you fail, you will not regret it. Definitely not," he said.

Building motorcycles in the giants' shadow

After winning, Zhang calmly observed in the March 30 interview: "We are still competing in the middleweight class, where everyone's bikes face restrictions. The premier class has fewer restrictions and is the most challenging. We are not in the very highest class yet. Think of this as high school; university is the next class we will challenge."

This view comes from his understanding of the industry. As a startup, ZX Moto's strengths are efficiency, cost, and a fearless exploratory spirit. But gaps remain against rivals with a century of accumulated experience.

"Their advantage is experience," he said. "If we encounter the same problem today, they look through their experience and immediately know the solution. We lack that experience, so we have to put many engineers onto finding one."

How, then, can they catch up?

His answer: "We buy all the world's leading products, dismantle them, and learn. But we do not replicate them; replication has no value. Our engineers must fully understand what the engineers who designed and manufactured them were thinking. Once we understand, it is as though that team has given us a lesson."

That is ZX Moto's learning logic: stand on giants' shoulders without becoming their shadow. Through this method, it accumulates experience and converts it into its own technical strengths.

Asked how someone with a junior secondary education leads PhD-level R&D teams, Zhang answered openly: "A PhD may focus on one narrow specialty. I cannot focus on only one; I need comprehensive understanding. Then professional engineers, or groups of engineers, help me complete each specialty."

"Where does my expertise come from? Decades continuously doing this work. Learn one thing a day, and over time you could become a PhD too," he said.

He says he never lies because he follows a logic almost like that of martial-arts novels: lying would dissipate his vital spirit. That spirit sustains his decades of undistracted motorcycle study. Lose it, and a person collapses.

In ZX Moto's Douyin livestreams, he often handles after-sales problems personally.

Once, he lost his temper when colleagues were slow to resolve an issue, then reflected on camera: "I am not angry; my voice was just a little loud."

This style of "boss handling after-sales online with a temper" has actually earned fans' trust. They see a craftsman serious about products and user needs, rather than a remote executive.

Figures disclosed at ZX Moto's 2026 partner conference and other official company data show 2025 output value of RMB 750 million and R&D spending of RMB 69.58 million, or 9.33%. The company completed RMB 90 million in Series A financing in January 2026 at a post-money valuation of RMB 1.09 billion, registering the corporate changes in early March.

Who recognized ZX Moto's potential and invested early? Its final two listed shareholders are Jinhua Zhechuang Jinyi Zhikong Venture Capital Partnership (Limited Partnership), with 5.50%, and Hangzhou Zhechuang Baige Venture Capital Partnership (Limited Partnership), with 2.75%. Tracing their ownership reveals links to Zhejiang's provincial state-assets regulator.

"China's greatest strengths, I think, are efficiency and cost," Zhang said candidly. "We are building that experience now. Our efficiency is high, costs low, and manufacturing capability now matches Europe and Japan. Their advantage is experience. We are using ten times the effort to make up lost time."

The 2026 partner conference disclosed that the company's first phase had designed annual capacity of 20,000 units in 2025, while actual annual sales were 25,000, implying 125% capacity utilization and overloaded production.

On March 21, 2026, preorders opened for the 500RR and 820RR. The company's official presale report showed 5,543 firm orders within 100 hours; the 820RR alone secured 1,986 confirmed orders in twelve hours. Following the March 28–29 double victory in Portugal, orders surged further: 820RR orders rose 158% in twenty-four hours and 200% over three days in Chongqing. Current 820RR delivery time is around four weeks, with customers queuing and paying in full at stores in multiple locations.

Calculations based on the 2026 partner conference figures show that, in 2025, first-phase designed annual capacity was 20,000 units against actual annual sales of 25,000. Utilization reached 125%, meaning the company was already producing beyond capacity.

Sparks, circuits, and the future

On March 30, the interview at ZX Moto headquarters in Chongqing neared its end. The journalists expected him to return to dreams, technology, and markets, as in previous interviews. Instead, he surprised everyone.

"What has given me the greatest sense of achievement in my life?" His voice softened. "Honestly, my son. He was born in 2012."

It contrasted sharply with the stubborn, tough image he had previously presented publicly.

For his son's future, he said: "My requirements are very low: pass exams, commit no crimes, hurt nobody, and become the person he wants to be. That would already be perfect." He admitted once wanting to force his son into racing as his own unfinished dream. Now he no longer does: "If he wants to train, I take him. If he does not, we do not."

At that moment, the motorcycle fanatic who cries at races, shouts in livestreams, and insists on precision in R&D shed his halo and became an ordinary father.

Asked about the future, his answer was concise and firm: "Make ZX Moto world-class in ten years." He does not regard the present as success: "I am only just starting. Really, just starting."

On March 30, a motorcycle racer told Economic Observer that Zhang was not alone on this track, because "he has lit a spark in countless racers' hearts."

The rider added that Zhang had allowed Chinese racers like himself to "see the light" in every corner and straight.

(Author: Wang Yajie)
