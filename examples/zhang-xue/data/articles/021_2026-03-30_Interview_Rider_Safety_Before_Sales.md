## Interview with ZX Moto Founder Zhang Xue: For Riders' Safety, I Would Rather Lose 10% of Sales

Jimu News reporter Deng Bo

On March 28–29, Chinese brand ZX Moto achieved a historic breakthrough in the middleweight class at the Portuguese round of the Superbike World Championship (WSBK). French rider Valentin Debise rode the ZX Moto 820RR-RS to victory in both races, securing back-to-back wins. It was the first time a Chinese motorcycle manufacturer had reached the top in this class of an elite international motorcycle championship.

In fact, ZX Moto was established only a little over two years ago. Its founder, Zhang Xue, comes from Mayang, Huaihua, Hunan, and has loved motorcycles since childhood. A documentary shows the then-19-year-old Zhang chasing a film crew for more than 100 kilometers to secure an opportunity to join a professional racing team. In 2025, the company's first model, the 500RR, ranked third in sales among individual models. The road-going version of the championship-winning 820RR had been available for preorder for only a little over ten days but had already secured more than 3,500 confirmed orders.

On March 30, at the factory of Chongqing Zhangxue Motorcycle Industry Co., Ltd., Zhang told Jimu News about his journey.

ZX Moto speaks to the media.

Reporter: Tell us about your entrepreneurial experience. What was the greatest difficulty?

Zhang Xue: After the Spring Festival in 2013, I went to Chongqing, a motorcycle-industry hub, with RMB 20,000 in cash. At first, I helped modify motorcycles built using generic molds and sold them. I sold over a hundred and made more than RMB 100,000. After others in the industry copied the idea, I sold motorcycles on Taobao, doing the graphics, customer service, and after-sales support myself. In one year, I sold 200 motorcycles on the platform, became number one in the Taobao category, and made several hundred thousand yuan. That gave me the money to develop motorcycle molds.

It took a year to go from mold development to the first completed motorcycle. We were feeling our way across the river, stone by stone. We promoted the new bike online, and within a little over ten minutes of listing it, we had sold 50. Within a few days we had sold 500. It was completely unexpected. But with so many sold, we had no money to buy supplies. I borrowed from fellow riders and approached crowdfunding platforms. A motorcycle developed as cheaply as possible is bound to have quality problems. We sold perhaps 700 or 800, but that first bike failed, and we lost money. We continued developing a second bike, and an investor approached me. I then helped establish Kove. In 2024, because of personality-related issues and other reasons, I left to establish ZX Moto.

A lot happened, but honestly, I never felt any particular thing was especially difficult. The hardest part was stepping out the door and setting off for Chongqing. Building motorcycles with RMB 20,000 was a joke in itself. Nobody at the time thought it could be done.

Reporter: Twenty years ago, you rode 100 kilometers chasing journalists just to get an opportunity. Tell us what happened. Looking back from where you are now, how do you see your younger self?

Zhang Xue: That television program had, in today's language, "a lot of traffic." I told them to interview me because I was an incredible rider. In fact, my skills were not good at the time; I tricked the journalists into coming. They filmed for a while, thought there was nothing special, and lost interest. I just would not let them go. When they went elsewhere to film other things, I said, "I'll come with you."

Even now, I think my mindset is still much the same. I was thick-skinned then. These days I might give a little more thought to embarrassment and that sort of thing.

ZX Moto production line.

Reporter: How did you feel the moment a ZX Moto crossed the finish line to win a high-level international race? What does it mean for a Chinese-made motorcycle to win a world championship race?

Zhang Xue: My emotions were incredibly complicated, churning inside me. I do not know how to describe them. This victory will give the entire industry, upstream and downstream, greater confidence in what we are doing. Our country's automotive supply chain is extremely comprehensive. Over the past ten years, our equipment, processes, manufacturing capability, and standards have improved enormously. Any component on a vehicle—even any component on a MotoGP bike or an F1 car—can absolutely be made in China if we have the drawings, and it will definitely be no worse than those made in Europe, Japan, or the United States.

What we really lack now is a sufficiently extensive store of experience. But that is all right. When a problem arises, the whole team throws itself into solving it. If we cannot solve it in a day, we can in a week. If we cannot solve it in a week, we can in a month. That is how we completed our 820.

Another effect is that foreigners may reconsider how they see us. When we first arrived at the circuit, people looked at us dismissively. Once we achieved good results, they spoke to us as equals. To put it bluntly, within the next five years, Chinese motorcycles will take more than 50% of the large-displacement motorcycle market currently held by so-called international brands.

The ZX Moto 820RR-RS in competition.

Reporter: ZX Moto finished four seconds ahead of other brands in this race, beating many internationally renowned names. What do those four seconds mean to ZX Moto?

Zhang Xue: It is just one race victory. Whether the gap was four seconds or two depends on many factors. The rider performed particularly well that day and was not disrupted. Then there are the modifications permitted within the rules: our bike has different settings, a different chassis, different tuning, and different handling. All of these build an advantage. Both the motorcycle's performance and the rider's performance contributed, and of course there was a little luck.

Reporter: Preorders for the road-going 820RR opened recently, but you prohibit purchases by customers with less than one year's riding experience. Why? Will that not affect sales?

Zhang Xue: I hope fewer people die. This is for users' safety. The rule will reduce this model's sales by at least 10%, but my company will not die without that 10%.

Outside the ZX Moto factory.

Reporter: Can you share any other racing plans?

Zhang Xue: We will take part in the Shanghai round of MX GP this September, and preparations have already begun. If, during my lifetime, I have not grown tired of doing this and we have enough money, we will enter MotoGP (the motorcycle world championship, the highest level of road motorcycle racing organized by the FIM, known as "F1 on two wheels").

Reporter: How are ZX Moto's sales and production doing? Why do you think people like the brand?

Zhang Xue: Our suppliers cannot keep up with component demand. Moving factories increased our capacity. We used to build a little over 100 bikes a day, and now we can build more than 200. We are building a second production line, and once it starts operating, output may approach 300 a day. My suppliers have not ramped up yet, so right now we work while we wait. We have more than 4,000 orders for the 500RR and more than 3,000 for the 820RR. (Reporter's note: According to ZX Moto staff, since reservations opened on March 21, the new 500RR has secured more than 4,500 confirmed preorders, and the 820RR more than 3,500.)

Users like our exhaust note, horsepower, and lightweight handling. I have always said that when users ride a motorcycle, they can feel whether its engineers and designers put their hearts into it. I am very confident that anyone who test-rides our bikes will place an order.

ZX Moto WSBK results. (Image source: ZX Moto's official WeChat account.)

Reporter: After so many years as an entrepreneur, how would you explain your success? What advice would you offer young people?

Zhang Xue: The reason for my success is that I have done what I wanted to do, regardless of the outcome, for decades. No matter how difficult things get, I never think of giving up. If the work does not kill me, I work as hard as I possibly can. My advice to young people is: if you are not short of money, follow your heart and do what you want to do. If your finances are limited, first find a practical way to earn money.
