# Called the "Lei Jun of Motorcycles": Champion Zhang Xue's Life in the Fast Lane

> Source: [China Entrepreneur (Hu Nannan)](https://m.chinaventure.com.cn/news/78-20260331-390740.html)

## Called the "Lei Jun of Motorcycles": Champion Zhang Xue's Life in the Fast Lane

### He simply wants to follow his own path with single-minded determination: "If I do not like it, I change it."

Zhang Xue waved a red flag excitedly: "I have waited twenty years for this moment... We won!"

On March 28, ZX Moto won at WSBK's Portuguese round by nearly four seconds, becoming China's first manufacturer to win an international medium-to-large-displacement motorcycle event. Meanwhile, at the WSBK viewing carnival on Chongqing's Guangyang Island, Zhang wept with joy.

After the win, he posted a video: "Sometimes, when you do something out of love rather than for the outcome, perhaps the result really will be different."

It took him twenty years to reach this moment.

Born in a small mountain village in Huaihua, Hunan, in 1987, Zhang loved motorcycles as a child—"loved them beyond words," he told China Entrepreneur. Without money, he had to take an indirect route toward becoming a racer. After graduating from junior secondary school in 2003, he became a motorcycle-repair apprentice; at seventeen, he opened his own shop. Over the next twenty-plus years, his entire life revolved around motorcycles: full-time racing, working for a motorcycle company, assembling and selling bikes, and eventually starting a company to build his own.

His mind holds just one thing, motorcycles: "I do not think about anything else."

On his desk sits a palm-sized sign reading "Do not shout," bought for him by the head of the company's general affairs department to encourage him to lose his temper less often.

He is famous in the industry for his temper. Before joining Zhangxue Motorcycle Industry Co., Ltd. (ZX Moto), one employee heard, "Mr. Zhang is very strict and shouts at people at the slightest thing," and was warned to be careful. Working with him, however, the employee felt: "He focuses on the issue, not the person. It is not really scolding; his voice is just a little loud." An R&D employee described how Zhang would be smiling before entering the R&D room, then turn completely serious once inside.

On departmental inspections, he appears unexpectedly behind someone's workstation to watch them work. Sometimes he points at the screen and begins a discussion; sometimes he suddenly raises his voice so the entire office can hear—he has lost his temper again.

"I reckon most people working with me have been hurt by me to some degree, because I never soften what I say. I do not think about saving people's feelings; when I challenge someone, I do it directly," Zhang said. He recognizes the problem, even seeing it as "a kind of incompetence," but it is instinctive: "When it happens and you have not held it back, what can you do?"

He showed this trait during our interview too. In January this year, China Entrepreneur interviewed him at ZX Moto's Chongqing headquarters. Answering a question, he might suddenly speak loudly, then soften his tone seconds later. That is one side of the coin.

The other is his single-mindedness, a trait evident from early on.

A widely circulated video shows nineteen-year-old Zhang riding through rain for three hours after a television crew's vehicle, hoping they would film him riding so a team might see him and let him become a racer. For him, it was the only route. A team eventually did contact him, and he became a rider.

Many ZX Moto employees are motorcycle enthusiasts. Some changed industries; others moved to Chongqing from around the country, most drawn by Zhang himself. One employee attributed his prominence to his very distinct personal brand. Online users call him "the Lei Jun of motorcycles": both an influencer with a million followers in motorcycle circles and a "motorcycle big shot" who builds bikes.

He is also stubborn about anything he believes in.

Before starting his business, Zhang spent several years as an employee. "I challenged everyone in the company, including my managers and boss. I was like a workplace idiot," he said, laughing at his early working years. His challenges were not merely verbal. If a manager rejected his product-design concept, he would say OK, then use colleagues' two-hour lunch break to build a model for the manager to try, proving his point with results.

In 2017, he co-founded Kove with two other founders; it is now a well-known Chinese motorcycle company. In March 2024, he announced on WeChat Moments that he was leaving without another job lined up. The following month, he founded a brand bearing his own name, ZX Moto. He wrote that his departure reflected a divergence between his dream and investors' interests.

He did not want to discuss those differences at length. Asked about them, his expression changed immediately. After a few seconds, he said: "I do not want to judge other people now... We disagreed, and I could no longer get what I wanted, so I could only follow my own path."

He simply wants to follow his path single-mindedly. "If I do not like it, I change it," he told China Entrepreneur.

Every Spring Festival holiday, he organizes a motorcycle tour, usually with a dozen or so riders, through Hunan, Guangxi, Guangdong, and elsewhere. This year marks the eleventh. A long holiday is rare, he feels: "It would be such a waste not to spend it riding."

He has no other hobbies. Every day he rides his small scooter to and from work. An employee told China Entrepreneur that he still has no car-driving license and cannot drive a car.

He does not even want to be an entrepreneur. "I do not like entrepreneurship either. I like building and developing motorcycles," he said with a laugh. "What I enjoy most is the process of making bikes. That is what I really enjoy."

As online users joke, he builds motorcycles because he is "hopelessly hooked," rather than to make money.

Below is China Entrepreneur's conversation with Zhang Xue, edited for length:

Zhang's window of opportunity is three years

China Entrepreneur: When you launched ZX Moto's first model last March, you said this was your last chance and that you had staked everything. Has ZX Moto won by now?

Zhang Xue: We survived. Last year's results were very impressive, although we reached only 85% of our target. We aimed to produce 30,000 in 2025 but made just over 25,000. Without production-capacity problems and failures in the supply of a few parts, we really could have reached 30,000.

China Entrepreneur: What were sales last year?

Zhang Xue: RMB 720 million.

China Entrepreneur: What about profit?

Zhang Xue: Negative RMB 20 million. [Laughs.] As we say, you work like a tiger, then tally the year-end accounts and find you have lost RMB 25 million.

China Entrepreneur: What is your goal now?

Zhang Xue: To make Zhang Xue Motorcycles a global top-ten brand. This year we rank eighth in China's recreational-motorcycle segment. Next year, fifth should be very easy, and third is quite possible.

China Entrepreneur: Is aiming for third domestically next year aggressive, given that the company is only two years old?

Zhang Xue: No. If we cannot even do that, this company is headed for death. A successful business takes only a few years to succeed. If it cannot, another twenty years will not change that. What company needs twenty years to develop? None. If it can make it, it emerges in a few years.

China Entrepreneur: What are your plans for this year?

Zhang Xue: At least 450 stores and RMB 2 billion in sales. At RMB 2 billion, we make a profit; RMB 1.5 billion is breakeven; below that, we lose money.

China Entrepreneur: Who are ZX Moto's competitors now?

Zhang Xue: Everyone, I suppose. [Laughs.] Saying that can easily offend peers, you know? Let me put it this way: CFMOTO led the industry in 2025. I think it is an excellent company. Its internationalization has gone very well in recent years, with very strong, rapid overseas growth. It is a model for us to learn from. Of course, I also want to surpass that model. That sounds less offensive, right?

China Entrepreneur: How many years do you expect that to take?

Zhang Xue: If we can surpass it, we will do so in three to five years. If we cannot in that time, we will not. Excellent people keep improving, and you either seize the window you are given or you do not. At most five years from founding, or at most three from today. If you can, you can; if you cannot, the opportunity will be gone.

China Entrepreneur: So ZX Moto has a three-year window?

Zhang Xue: Yes. Three years is the window for how good we can become. The survival window was last year. If I had not reached 20,000 bikes last year, I would have been finished. That is the basic volume. Falling short would have shown all kinds of problems, making failure natural.

China Entrepreneur: To succeed within that window, where must you excel, and what mistakes must you avoid?

Zhang Xue: We are building a quality-management system. Getting product definition wrong or having a particularly serious quality problem would be unbearable. If a product goes off course and its launch fails, the whole team's work for the year counts for nothing. The financial cost is relatively small; the biggest costs are time and opportunity. That year is gone; you cannot do it over. The industry then shuts its doors to you, suppliers lose trust, and dealers stop supporting and trusting you. We cannot afford that outcome now.

Borrowing RMB 7 million to pay wages

China Entrepreneur: Where did the money for this venture come from?

Zhang Xue: In our seed round, an investor put in RMB 20 million for 20%. I privately borrowed another RMB 10 million from him, making RMB 30 million. I also received some money when I left Kove, paid in three installments; the first was several million. Altogether that gave me over RMB 30 million. I calculated every payment, expecting it to last precisely until our first model launched in March 2025. But plans often cannot keep up with changes.

By February last year, we were already short. We had no money for February wages, so I borrowed everywhere—from friends, industry peers, suppliers, even RMB 1 million from our landlord. Altogether I borrowed RMB 7 million to pay wages. After our March launch and the start of deliveries, money came in. Supplier payment terms and dealer deposits gave us a pool of funds.

China Entrepreneur: The period before the first launch was therefore very difficult.

Zhang Xue: Yes, and I absolutely had to launch in March. Otherwise, the company would run out of money. The hardest thing for a company is a broken funding chain, right? Once that breaks, you fail whether or not you accept it.

China Entrepreneur: Were you anxious about money?

Zhang Xue: No. There was pressure: pushing myself to the limit, accepting no excuses. Whatever happens, today's task must be finished, even if we work until dawn.

China Entrepreneur: Are funding problems still an issue?

Zhang Xue: No. We just secured RMB 90 million in January 2026, a Series A from Zhejiang Venture Capital. What does that money do? It gives me more ammunition. With ammunition, you become bolder and sleep more soundly. [Chuckles.]

China Entrepreneur: Did you approach them? How long did negotiations take?

Zhang Xue: We did not seek them out; outsiders approached us. Talks took about three months, quite fast. Zhejiang is highly efficient in every respect, I think.

China Entrepreneur: What stake did RMB 90 million buy?

Zhang Xue: Around 8.5%. The round was calculated at a valuation of RMB 1 billion.

Work with everything you have to make it as good as possible

China Entrepreneur: You named this new brand after yourself. Are you concerned about future risks?

Zhang Xue: No. This is what I am devoting my whole life to. If I fail to do it well, my life has already failed, so why worry about that? If my life is already that lousy, would I still care about ruining the name Zhang Xue? Besides, can I fool myself about my own life? Impossible. This is everything to me. I will work with everything I have to make it as good as possible, without anyone needing to demand it.

China Entrepreneur: What is your greatest achievement in the two years since founding ZX Moto?

Zhang Xue: We have reshaped after-sales service in the motorcycle industry. Previously, if a store gave poor service, users might post online or complain. Now that is unnecessary. At least at ZX Moto, stores cannot refuse warranty coverage or make things difficult for you. They know the consequences would be very serious. We have basically changed the state of after-sales service. Peers can see what we have done, and they are gradually trying to do it too.

China Entrepreneur: Would that count as your contribution to the industry?

Zhang Xue: I think so.

China Entrepreneur: Do other motorcycle companies have founders personally communicating with users as you do?

Zhang Xue: Apparently not. They may put forward someone responsible for after-sales, quality, or sales, rather than the founder.

China Entrepreneur: Are you the first person in motorcycle circles to do this?

Zhang Xue: An oddball.

China Entrepreneur: Have you heard people call you the "Lei Jun of motorcycles" and ZX Moto the "Xiaomi of motorcycles"?

Zhang Xue: That is what internet users say.

China Entrepreneur: Do you agree?

Zhang Xue: I do not know. [Laughs.] First, I think Lei Jun is incredibly capable. He built cars quickly and often seems to be on the front line. That may be only what we see; we do not know the actual situation. But what we see is certainly good. When everyone involved in a project can reach the decision-maker, the team's effectiveness, efficiency, and quality improve greatly, without such traditional hierarchies. Maintaining that in a company of their size is itself very impressive.

Musk is even more remarkable. Can you imagine someone so wealthy and capable still sleeping in the workshop? It is extraordinary. If people so much better than us work even harder, what excuse do we have not to work hard or to lie flat?

China Entrepreneur: Which entrepreneur do you like most?

Zhang Xue: I used to like Lei Jun a lot.

China Entrepreneur: Not anymore?

Zhang Xue: [Laughs.] It is not that I dislike him now. I have a few points—can I say them? [Asks a colleague.] Maybe better not. No, no. [Then says them anyway.] If I were Lei Jun, during the vented-hood controversy, I would offer two choices: first, refund the hood's price difference; second, let those who had not taken delivery cancel. Handling those two things properly would greatly reduce grounds for criticism, without really affecting him, since orders are not a problem. How much would refunding all the hoods cost? Compared with reputation and impact, it would be negligible.

China Entrepreneur: Is resolving consumers' after-sales issues why you hold an online livestream every Wednesday?

Zhang Xue: I stream only one hour a week, which contributes very little directly to solving problems. You cannot solve much in an hour. But what does it do? First, oversight: nobody dares fob off users because they can speak directly with me. Also, I must maintain long-term communication with users. We make products, and users pay. They are the ones we really need to please—not superficially, but by winning them over through our products. Once users recognize the products, they naturally choose them, and people naturally come forward to become dealers.

I challenged everyone in the company, including my boss

China Entrepreneur: When did you decide motorcycles were what you wanted to do?

Zhang Xue: Junior secondary school. I have done this one thing all these years. Once I decide on something, I do not change. I have never thought of changing; it would be troublesome. As far back as I can remember, I knew I loved motorcycles.

China Entrepreneur: Did you learn repairs because you loved motorcycles and wanted to race? Was repair work the route to your dream?

Zhang Xue: Yes, the only route. [Pauses.] Put that way, I feel quite lucky. Sometimes having no choice is a blessing, is it not? Too many choices can leave you confused and torn.

China Entrepreneur: How much did you save during those years repairing bikes?

Zhang Xue: I repaired them for a little over three years. I graduated in 2003 and closed the shop at the end of 2006: a year and a half learning repairs, then a year and a half running the shop. I earned RMB 50,000–60,000.

China Entrepreneur: What did you do each day as a racer?

Zhang Xue: Repair, ride, repair, ride, repair, ride. And maintain the practice ground—taking a shovel and digging out a little track. A racer's life was fantastic.

China Entrepreneur: When did you realize you could not continue as a racer?

Zhang Xue: When the money ran out. To earn money racing, you need high placings. That is true globally: only top riders earn enough to support themselves. Chinese riders' earnings are low; perhaps only a few in each class make money. I was one who could not. If I had had even RMB 10,000 left, I could have continued for another six months or a year.

China Entrepreneur: Did you go to Chongqing after stopping racing?

Zhang Xue: I went to Zhejiang Apollo Motorcycle Company.

China Entrepreneur: Was that your first workplace job? How long were you there?

Zhang Xue: Yes. [Laughs.] Over three years. Do you know why I am laughing? Mentioning the workplace reminds me that I was like a workplace idiot—not a beginner, an idiot. I challenged everyone, including managers and my boss. It is funny looking back. [Laughs.]

China Entrepreneur: How did you challenge your boss?

Zhang Xue: Once, I explained to a superior how our suspension should be designed. He disagreed, and I said OK. At lunchtime, everyone went to eat, but I stayed. They would rest for about two hours afterward; by then, I had built a model. I had him try it so he could immediately feel the change in force. I built two adjustable configurations and said, "This is state A, this is state B. Try both yourself." After trying, he said, "Oh, so that is how it works. Right!" We then used my design.

China Entrepreneur: He had disagreed before that.

Zhang Xue: He disagreed with my direction and proposal, so I made a model. Building one is quick: whatever I envision, I can produce very rapidly.

China Entrepreneur: Were you promoted at Apollo?

Zhang Xue: Yes. I became a project manager and later left, because I believed the kind of work and products we are doing now was the right direction.

Thanks to the fellow who copied me

China Entrepreneur: After Apollo, you began assembling motorcycles in Chongqing. Was that your first business venture?

Zhang Xue: Yes. While assembling bikes, I realized I needed to establish my own barriers immediately. After just two batches, someone began copying me using exactly the same approach. That was when I realized I needed to design my own products.

But I should thank the fellow who copied me. Without him, I might have stayed where I was. His copying made me see the problem, and I changed immediately. So all those supposed enemies we meet are actually people helping us grow, right? Thinking about it that way makes quite a lot of sense.

I then used the hundred-thousand-plus yuan I had earned assembling bikes for design. I insisted this bike follow my own ideas, making imitation expensive—at least a million yuan. In 2015, I began developing my own product, called "Freedom."

China Entrepreneur: Why did you stop making Freedom?

Zhang Xue: Developing it cost over RMB 1 million, but the development was utterly inadequate. Now, a single set of body panels costs over RMB 1 million, even RMB 2 million; then I developed an entire bike for RMB 1 million. What does that mean? An extremely cheap, low-end supply chain. That was also our only option, since good suppliers had no interest in working with us. First-class suppliers choose their customers too.

China Entrepreneur: Does ZX Moto still face those supply-chain problems?

Zhang Xue: No. What is the biggest asset I have accumulated over these years? The industry has seen that Zhang Xue genuinely develops products. People believe I can make them well, so they are very willing to cooperate. When we first founded ZX Moto, money was extremely tight. Suppliers knew I had no money but still chose to work with us, even providing substantial help, such as support with development costs.

Resenting defeat is pointless: a loss is a loss

China Entrepreneur: When did the industry begin noticing you? During Kove?

Zhang Xue: Yes.

China Entrepreneur: What did Kove mean to you?

Zhang Xue: Training, in every respect.

China Entrepreneur: Did you leave because of differences in philosophy with investors?

Zhang Xue: I do not really want to judge others now; that would not look good. We disagreed. It was no longer what I wanted, and I could no longer get that, so I could only follow my own path.

China Entrepreneur: When did you think of starting another company?

Zhang Xue: About a month before leaving, perhaps a little over half a month. I proposed a project involving a three-cylinder engine, and it was rejected.

China Entrepreneur: Why?

Zhang Xue: I cannot read other people's minds. The stated reason was insufficient money to support it. But what was really happening? I had no authority. I could no longer define products, though I could still make suggestions, right? When even suggestions are not accepted, why keep agonizing? So I stopped agonizing.

China Entrepreneur: Did you feel unwilling to accept leaving, after so many years building the company?

Zhang Xue: No, though I was very reluctant to part with it. There was no refusal to accept it, really. If we fight and I lose, I can only accept that I lost.

China Entrepreneur: You quickly accepted what you could not change.

Zhang Xue: Once an outcome is settled, resentment is meaningless. My former Kove partner once said, "The market has got worse lately." I said, "Then it is worse. What good does worrying do? Neither you nor I can change it."

China Entrepreneur: What was the deepest pitfall you encountered?

Zhang Xue: Equity, probably. I knew little about it before, but after those experiences, I paid particular attention to equity in this venture, especially the threshold for control.

China Entrepreneur: Was that your biggest lesson from Kove?

Zhang Xue: Yes. Perhaps not a lesson exactly, but certainly gaining wisdom from experience.

I do not want to be an entrepreneur; I just like building motorcycles

China Entrepreneur: Does entrepreneurship fill you with passion?

Zhang Xue: Honestly, I do not want to be an entrepreneur, and I do not like entrepreneurship. [Laughs.] I like building and developing motorcycles.

China Entrepreneur: Entrepreneurship is just a way to realize your dream?

Zhang Xue: Yes. I really do not have especially grand ambitions. I set a global top-ten target because the work requires a goal. But what I enjoy most is the process of making motorcycles, immersing myself in it.

I would absolutely not make my whole life excessively bitter and exhausting in exchange for greater achievement. I want to live in the way that feels best to me.

China Entrepreneur: I am curious: continuing one thing purely from passion and dreams may be difficult for ordinary people.

Zhang Xue: Perhaps I am single-minded, so I do not think about other things. I do not want to spend energy on anything else. This is enough. I have no other hobbies: no cards, fishing, or video games.

China Entrepreneur: You reportedly have no car-driving license and cannot drive. Are you uninterested in cars?

Zhang Xue: I do not particularly need one and have no desire to drive. Taxis are convenient and cheaper.

China Entrepreneur: Is building motorcycles your life's mission or goal?

Zhang Xue: That sounds too grand; I would not elevate it to a mission. Frankly and clearly, it is about enjoying myself. But since we are doing it, let us do it as well as possible. I cannot focus solely on my own enjoyment; so many people need to earn a living too, right?

China Entrepreneur: Have you been able to enjoy everything you have done over the years?

Zhang Xue: Whatever I do not enjoy, I change.

China Entrepreneur: What made you unhappy?

Zhang Xue: For example, when I decided to leave Kove.

China Entrepreneur: Does your success owe more to effort or luck?

Zhang Xue: I do not count as successful yet, not at all. If you insist on using that word, perhaps success is: "Damn, I am doing what I enjoy."

China Entrepreneur: What qualities must an entrepreneur possess?

Zhang Xue: Focus. [Answers immediately.]

First, focus, and also rapid learning. We can never wait until we have learned everything before starting. I always say we cannot plan something perfectly before doing it. We must identify problems during the process and eliminate them quickly. Also, we need enough mental strength to withstand every crushing blow. I think these are relatively important.

China Entrepreneur: What is your most important trait?

Zhang Xue: In terms of what helps me most, it is that once I commit to something, I do not give up or change. No matter what mountains of problems and difficulties come, I cannot imagine changing or abandoning it—unless I am certain there is nothing more I can do. Then, OK, let me "lie down" for a while.

China Entrepreneur: Have you ever "lain down" in all these years?

Zhang Xue: No.

China Entrepreneur: Have you considered doing so in the future?

Zhang Xue: When I am exhausted, cannot keep working, or have reached my goal. If I have not reached it, I probably will not.
